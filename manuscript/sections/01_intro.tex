\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Phishing is still the most common initial-access vector, and its production cost has collapsed since instruction-tuned language models began to write fluent, personalised lures from a few lines of reconnaissance \cite{heiding2024,hazell2023}. In controlled studies, fully automated LLM spear-phishing matched or exceeded the click-through rates of expert-written messages \cite{heiding2026,bethany2025}, and generator--critic loops rewrite a lure until machine defenders stop flagging it \cite{qi2025}. For a detector at an e-mail gateway this has a practical consequence. The attacker can change the surface form of a campaign at negligible cost, by switching the generating model, paraphrasing a known lure or translating it, and the change reaches the gateway as an unannounced shift of the message distribution, long before verified labels for the new campaign exist.

Labels are the scarce resource in this setting. Each one is an analyst's verdict on a reported or quarantined message, it arrives with a delay, and a security team can afford to verify only a small share of the traffic. Fully supervised stream learners assume that every label eventually arrives; static detectors use none. The question that matters to an operator lies in between: given a budget of a few thousand verified labels, \emph{when} should they be bought? Two answers are common. Active-learning samplers spread the budget over the stream and pick uncertain messages \cite{zliobaite2014,escudero2025}; drift detectors on the error stream wait until enough labelled errors have accumulated \cite{bifet2007}. Neither uses the fact that a new generator or campaign moves the unlabelled message distribution at once.

This paper studies a third answer: detect the shift without labels and spend the budget in a burst right after it. SLIDE tests every batch for a change of the representation distribution with Kolmogorov--Smirnov tests on random one-dimensional projections, gated by a sliced-Wasserstein effect size, and buys the labels of the alarm window when both gates fire. We keep the method small on purpose. An earlier design of SLIDE added replay, an adversarial term, a proximal penalty and surprisal features to the update; none of them changed the result in the ablation reported here, and they were removed. What remains is the trigger, a level schedule that makes its false-alarm guarantee hold for the stream rather than for a single test, and a purchase rule that can feed any learner. The evaluation is built to test the trigger rather than the learner: equal-budget samplers for two learners, two further shift sources, three alternative stream designs, a stationary null stream, a real-drift probe and sensitivity grids over $K$, the label window and the label delay.

The contributions are four.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A label-free trigger with stream-level false-alarm control} (Section~\ref{sec:method}). Holm-corrected KS tests on random projections and on the learner's score are combined with a sliced-Wasserstein effect-size gate. A harmonic level schedule bounds the probability of any false alarm in a reference epoch by $\alpha$ under arbitrary overlap between test windows (Proposition~\ref{prop:fwer}).
\item \textbf{Label-on-alarm acquisition as a learner-agnostic policy} (Section~\ref{sec:policy}). The same alarms drive the update of a transformer head or of an online TF-IDF learner, so the value of the timing can be separated from the value of the learner.
\item \textbf{An equal-budget evaluation of when to buy labels} (Sections~\ref{sec:setup} and~\ref{sec:results}). On a 36{,}000-message stream with seven segments, including LLM-generated, paraphrased and machine-translated phishing, SLIDE is compared at equal label cost with uniform, uncertainty, random-time and oracle-time purchasing for both learners, and with fully supervised learners that consume every label.
\item \textbf{An account of what the trigger cannot do} (Sections~\ref{sec:realdrift}--\ref{sec:discussion}). We measure false alarms on a stationary stream, the blind spot for real drift and a five-label-per-batch audit that closes it, the cost of the 128-token truncation together with a windowed scoring fix, and the false-positive rate on LLM-written legitimate mail.
\end{enumerate}

In brief, the trigger reached MCC 0.889 with 3{,}000 labels, against 0.818 for uniform and 0.794 for uncertainty sampling at the same cost and 0.891 for an oracle that knows the boundaries; with the TF-IDF learner the corresponding values were 0.885, 0.829, 0.834 and 0.887. An online TF-IDF learner that received all 35{,}500 labels remained the most accurate method (0.904). The remainder follows the order of the contributions, with the limits in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.
