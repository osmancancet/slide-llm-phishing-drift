\section{Experimental setup}\label{sec:setup}

\subsection{Corpora}
All data are public. Human-written traffic comes from the unified release \cite{ds_seven} of the seven corpora curated by Champa et al.\ \cite{champa2024}: Enron and Ling-Spam with SpamAssassin (1999--2004), TREC-05/06/07 (2005--2007) and CEAS-08 (2008). Their positive class is the union of phishing and spam, which we call \emph{malicious} and discuss in Section~\ref{sec:validity}. LLM-written traffic comes from three independent releases: \emph{GPTGen} \cite{ds_gptgen}, English phishing and legitimate messages generated with OpenAI models; \emph{SpearGen} \cite{ds_spear}, personalised spear-phishing generated from a name, a job title and a list of recent activities; and \emph{PhishFuzzer} \cite{toth2025phishfuzzer,ds_phishfuzzer}, phishing and valid messages generated from seed e-mails with controlled entities and lengths. PhishFuzzer releases six rewrites of every seed; we keep one rewrite per seed, chosen at random, so that no seed occurs twice. The last source is a Spanish release \cite{ds_spanish} in which the e-mail bodies of the spear-phishing, real phishing, Enron/SpamAssassin and hard-ham collections of Nahmias et al.\ \cite{nahmias2024} were machine-translated with an LLM; every class is translated, so the language changes while the translation process is shared by both classes. We remove inline sender addresses from GPTGen, strip HTML from the Spanish bodies, normalise whitespace, prefix every message with its subject line, discard very short messages and remove exact duplicates across all sources. Disjoint pools are then drawn with a fixed seed (Tables~\ref{tab:pools} and~\ref{tab:rev-pools}); no text appears in more than one pool.

\subsection{Streams}
\paragraph{Main stream} Each realisation ($r=0,\dots,9$) samples seven segments without replacement and shuffles the messages inside each segment: A (6{,}000 human messages, 1999--2004), B (6{,}000, TREC), C (6{,}000, CEAS-08), D (6{,}000: GPTGen and SpearGen phishing at 30\% prevalence with GPTGen legitimate mail and held-out human legitimate mail), E (4{,}000: a campaign of about 1{,}500 T1-paraphrased messages, 1{,}250 of them malicious, plus 2{,}500 human legitimate messages), F (4{,}000: 30\% PhishFuzzer phishing, 30\% PhishFuzzer valid mail and 40\% unused human legitimate mail) and G (4{,}000 Spanish messages, 30\% phishing). The stream has 36{,}000 messages in $b=250$-message batches. A$\to$B$\to$C are temporal and source shifts, C$\to$D and E$\to$F are generator shifts, D$\to$E is an adversarial shift and F$\to$G a language shift. Segment F is the first in which LLM-written legitimate mail is as frequent as LLM-written phishing, which tests directly whether a detector has learned authorship instead of intent. The warm-up window (4{,}000 messages from the 1999--2004 corpora) is the only data used to fine-tune the encoder and to fit the standardisation, and every initial learner is trained on it alone.

\paragraph{Stream designs} A fixed order of predefined segments can favour a detector tuned to that order \cite{souza2020}. We therefore also run three variants, five realisations each: \emph{gradual}, where every boundary is a 2{,}000-message zone in which the probability of drawing from the next segment rises linearly from 0 to 1; \emph{variable length}, where each segment keeps a uniformly drawn 50--100\% of its nominal length; and \emph{permuted}, where A stays first and the order of B--G is drawn at random per realisation.

\paragraph{Stationary null stream} To measure false alarms where none should occur, a stream of 20{,}000 messages is drawn i.i.d.\ from the union of pools A, B and C (20 realisations). Every alarm on it is a false alarm.

\paragraph{Real-drift probe} A label-free test cannot see a change of $P(Y\mid X)$ that leaves $P(X)$ intact. To measure this blind spot we draw a labelled warm-up set and two halves R0 and R1 of 4{,}000 messages each from the same pool of unused TREC mail, so that $P(X)$ is identical throughout. In R1, the legitimate messages of one region of $\phi$ (the $k$-means cell, $k=8$, closest to one fifth of the legitimate mail) are relabelled as malicious, which models a sender population that turns malicious without changing how it writes. Ten realisations are run. SLIDE+Audit adds $n_a\in\{5,10,25\}$ uniformly drawn audit labels per batch, whose prequential errors feed an ADWIN test ($\delta=0.002$); an ADWIN alarm buys the same 500-label window as a drift alarm.

\paragraph{Labels and delays} The labels of batch $t$ arrive at $t+D_t$. The default is $D_t=2$ for every batch; Section~\ref{sec:delay} varies it to 1, 4, 8 and 16 batches and to random delays drawn uniformly from $\{1,\dots,8\}$ and $\{1,\dots,16\}$ per batch.

\subsection{Compared methods}
Table~\ref{tab:methods} lists the methods, grouped by the labels they consume. Static detectors never see stream labels. The fully supervised methods receive every label after its delay. The label-on-alarm methods buy labels only when their trigger fires. Every head-based method uses the same frozen representation and the same head architecture and loss, so that the comparison isolates when and how many labels are used.

\paragraph{Equal-budget controls} The central question is whether the trigger spends labels better than a sampler with the same budget. SLIDE bought 3{,}000 labels per stream on average, which is 21 labels per batch over the 144 batches. Four samplers spend the same number. Budget-Uniform buys 21 random labels of every batch, and Budget-Uncertainty buys the 21 messages of every batch whose score is closest to 0.5 \cite{zliobaite2014}; both update their learner whenever labels arrive. Budget-Random buys six windows of 500 labels at batches drawn at random, and Budget-Oracle buys a window as soon as 500 messages of each new segment have arrived, which requires knowledge of the true boundaries that no deployed detector has. Each sampler is run with the head and with the TF-IDF learner, so that SLIDE and SLIDE-TFIDF each have four equal-budget counterparts.

\subsection{Hyper-parameters and statistical protocol}
Hyper-parameters (Table~\ref{tab:hyper}) were fixed before the stream experiments from defaults in the cited works and from the warm-up window; none was selected on stream data. Sections~\ref{sec:K}--\ref{sec:delay} vary $K$, $n_\ell$ and the delay.

We report precision, recall, MCC, PR-AUC, the false-positive rate (FPR) and the number of stream labels consumed. Stream-level metrics pool all prequential predictions of a realisation; we report the mean over realisations with a 95\% Student-$t$ confidence interval. Following Dem\v{s}ar \cite{demsar2006}, the realisation is the test unit: a Friedman test across methods with realisations as blocks, the Nemenyi critical difference at $\alpha=0.05$, and two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank tests of SLIDE against each method on the ten paired stream-level MCC values with Holm correction. With ten pairs the smallest exact two-sided Wilcoxon $p$-value is $2/2^{10}\approx0.002$; after Holm correction over 18 comparisons the smallest attainable adjusted value is 0.0352. Effect sizes are matched-pairs rank-biserial correlations $r_{rb}\in[-1,1]$.

\subsection{Computing environment and reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}
All experiments ran on one Intel Xeon processor at 2.10\,GHz with four cores, 15\,GB of RAM and no GPU, with Python~3, PyTorch 2.14 (CPU), Hugging Face \texttt{transformers}, scikit-learn, SciPy and \texttt{river}. The encoder is \texttt{distilroberta-base} \cite{sanh2019,liu2019roberta}, the paraphraser a T5-base model fine-tuned for paraphrasing \cite{hf_paraphraser}, and the similarity encoder \texttt{all-MiniLM-L6-v2}. Every number in this paper is read from files that the released pipeline writes; fixed seeds reproduce the pools and stream orderings. Code, per-run results and the manuscript source are available under the MIT licence at \url{https://github.com/osmancancet/slide-llm-phishing-drift}.
