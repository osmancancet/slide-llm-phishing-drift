\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Accuracy and label cost}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:rev-main} gives the stream-level results. The static detectors end between MCC 0.664 and 0.702, and the label-on-alarm and fully supervised methods lie between 0.867 and 0.904. SLIDE reaches 0.889 with 3{,}000 labels, which is 8.5\% of the 35{,}500 labels that the fully supervised methods consume. The online TF-IDF learner is the most accurate method (0.904) and ranks first in every realisation; SLIDE is 0.015 MCC below it and loses all ten paired realisations ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0352$, Table~\ref{tab:rev-stats}). ADWIN-triggered retraining of the same head with all labels reaches 0.891, which SLIDE does not separate from (3 wins out of ten, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.48$).

\input{tables/rev_main.tex}

The earlier design with the four-term objective and surprisal features reaches 0.888, statistically indistinguishable from the revised SLIDE ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.42$) at 8.4 times the update time. KSWIN on the score spends 9{,}700 labels in 19.4 purchases and ends 0.022 MCC below SLIDE (9/10, $p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0352$). The Friedman test rejects equal performance across the 19 methods ($\chi^2_F=166.7$); with a Nemenyi critical difference of 8.85 ranks, SLIDE (mean rank 4.8) is separated from the static detectors and from the uniform, uncertainty and random-time samplers with the head, but not from the other adaptive methods.

\input{tables/rev_stats.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:rev-segments} locates the differences. On the human segments A--C, SLIDE is within 0.02 MCC of the best method in each. The gap to the online TF-IDF learner is concentrated in the LLM segment D (0.818 against 0.892) and in the PhishFuzzer segment F (0.842 against 0.895). In both, the TF-IDF learner receives every label from the first batch of the segment on, while SLIDE scores with the previous head until its purchased window arrives. SLIDE-TFIDF, which buys the same labels at the same alarms but updates the TF-IDF learner, closes most of the segment-F gap (0.892) and a third of the segment-D gap (0.841). Figure~\ref{fig:rev-trajectory} shows the batch-level picture: the head-based methods dip at the first batches of D and F and recover once labels arrive, and the static head stays near MCC 0.2--0.4 on the LLM segments.

\subsection{When to buy labels: equal-budget controls}\label{sec:budgetctrl}
The equal-budget rows of Table~\ref{tab:rev-main} hold the learner and the number of labels fixed and vary only when the labels are bought. With the head, spreading 3{,}000 labels uniformly over the stream gives 0.818, choosing the most uncertain messages gives 0.794 and buying six windows at random batches gives 0.774. SLIDE exceeds each of them in all ten realisations by 0.071, 0.095 and 0.115 MCC ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0352$ each, the smallest value the design allows). An oracle that buys a window as soon as 500 messages of each new segment exist reaches 0.891, 0.002 above SLIDE and not significantly so ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.32$). The same ordering holds with the TF-IDF learner: uniform 0.829, uncertainty 0.834, random times 0.756, SLIDE-TFIDF 0.885 and oracle 0.887. The trigger thus captures almost all of the value that knowledge of the true boundaries would give, for either learner, and the samplers that ignore the shift lose 0.05--0.13 MCC at the same cost.

The uniform sampler loses most on the LLM segment D (0.618 against 0.818 for SLIDE). Twenty-one labels per batch arrive throughout D, but three epochs on each small set evidently do not move the head as far from the human-mail regime as one update on 500 labels of the new segment. Uncertainty sampling is worse than uniform sampling with the head (0.471 on D). This is consistent with a head that is confidently wrong on the new LLM traffic, so that the messages near a score of 0.5 are not the ones it misclassifies.

\subsection{Ablation and retention}\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:rev-ablation} removes or replaces one component at a time. Adaptation is necessary: without it the detector stays at the static level (0.664). The projections carry the trigger: a test on the score alone raises fewer alarms, buys 1{,}300 labels and ends at 0.828 ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.0215$), while the projections-only test reproduces SLIDE. Either gate alone raises the same alarms on this stream; on stationary traffic the SW gate alone does not (Section~\ref{sec:null}). None of the update variants changes stream MCC measurably: the full four-term objective with or without $\psi$, warm start with replay, warm start without replay and retraining from scratch all lie within 0.005 MCC of the revised configuration ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}\geq0.053$). The surprisal features $\psi$ add nothing (0.890 with, 0.889 without).

\input{tables/rev_ablation.tex}

Prequential MCC measures accuracy on the traffic at hand, not what a detector keeps from earlier campaigns. Table~\ref{tab:rev-retention} measures retention directly on a held-out pool from the sources of segments B--D. Every learner that updates only on recent data forgets: just before segment E SLIDE detects 0.937 of the pool, at the end of the stream 0.659; ADWIN-Retrain falls from 0.938 to 0.530 and the oracle schedule from 0.907 to 0.651. Class-balanced replay removes the loss. With replay SLIDE keeps 0.941 at the end, the earlier design keeps 0.948, and the online TF-IDF learner, which never discards its weights, keeps 0.794. Stream MCC cannot show this effect, because the stream never returns to an earlier source. Retention has a price on the current traffic. SLIDE+replay reaches stream MCC 0.884 (Table~\ref{tab:rev-main}), 0.005 below SLIDE in 9 of ten realisations ($p_{\mathrm{Holm}}=0.041$), at 2.4 times the update time, because part of every update is spent on earlier sources. The choice between the two is an operating decision: SLIDE when only the current traffic matters, SLIDE+replay when old campaigns may return.

\subsection{Number of projections and label window}\label{sec:K}
Table~\ref{tab:rev-K} varies the number of random projections. With $K=2$ the test misses 23\% of the boundaries and SLIDE ends at MCC 0.868; from $K=8$ on it misses at most one boundary in 30, and from $K=64$ on none. Stream MCC is flat between $K=32$ (0.887) and $K=512$ (0.887), and the mean detection delay stays between 0.5 and 0.7 batches over that range. The test time per stream grows from 14\,s at $K=2$ to 32\,s at the default $K=64$ and 126\,s at $K=512$. No setting raised an alarm inside a segment. The share of individually significant projections falls from 0.43 to 0.17 as $K$ grows, so a shift moves a minority of directions and more projections mainly add directions that the shift does not touch; $K=64$ is the smallest value without misses.

\input{tables/rev_K.tex}

Table~\ref{tab:rev-budget} varies the label window $n_\ell$ bought at each alarm. Below one batch ($n_\ell<250$) the window is a random subset of the alarm batch. With $n_\ell=50$ SLIDE spends 300 labels and reaches 0.820; with $n_\ell=100$, 600 labels and 0.853; with one batch, 0.871. Fifty labels per alarm, 300 in all, already match uniform sampling with 2{,}982 labels (0.818, Table~\ref{tab:rev-main}). Doubling the default window to 1{,}000 labels per alarm adds 0.002 MCC for 3{,}000 more labels. The TF-IDF learner needs fewer labels per alarm: SLIDE-TFIDF reaches 0.881 with one batch per alarm and gains 0.004 from doubling it.

\subsection{Label delay}\label{sec:delay}
Every method that learns from labels loses accuracy as labels arrive later (Table~\ref{tab:rev-delay}), because the head scores each new segment with the old model until the purchased window is delivered. SLIDE falls from 0.898 at $D=1$ to 0.831 at $D=8$ and 0.745 at $D=16$. The fully supervised online TF-IDF learner falls at the same rate, from 0.913 to 0.828 and 0.739, so its advantage over SLIDE disappears once labels are eight or more batches (2{,}000 messages) late. With random delays the gap remains: 0.819 for SLIDE against 0.862 for the online learner when delays range over 1--16 batches, because the online learner uses each early label as soon as it arrives, while SLIDE waits for the whole window. The uniform equal-budget sampler stays below SLIDE at every delay (0.837 at $D=1$, 0.692 at $D=16$).

\subsection{Stream designs}\label{sec:designs}
The ordering of Table~\ref{tab:rev-main} does not drive the result (Table~\ref{tab:rev-designs}). SLIDE ends at 0.886 on gradual boundaries, 0.873 with variable segment lengths and 0.875 with permuted segments, against 0.888 on the default stream. On gradual boundaries it buys 3{,}600 labels instead of 3{,}000, because some mixing zones raise a second alarm. It also ends above the oracle schedule (0.864), probably because the oracle buys its window as soon as 500 messages of the new segment exist, when the new segment is still a minority of the traffic in the mixing zone. Under every design the equal-budget samplers stay below SLIDE: uniform sampling with the head by 0.07--0.08 MCC and buying windows at random times by 0.08--0.13. The online TF-IDF learner with all labels remains the most accurate method under every design (0.887--0.919).

\subsection{False alarms on a stationary stream}\label{sec:null}
On the null stream every alarm is false and every purchased window is wasted (Table~\ref{tab:rev-null}). Each realisation runs about 75 drift tests. With the per-test level $\alpha$ of the earlier design, 10\% of the streams raise at least one false alarm, and with the KS gate alone 45\%, at a cost of 375 labels per stream on average. With harmonic $\alpha$-spending no realisation raises a false alarm, with the two gates or with the KS gate alone. The SW gate on its own, with spending, still raises a false alarm in 15\% of the streams, which is the reason to keep both gates: on the A--G stream either gate alone raises the same alarms (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}), and on stationary traffic only the conjunction, or the KS gate, stays silent. KSWIN on the detector score raises 11.1 false alarms per stream and buys 5{,}525 labels for nothing. Twenty realisations bound the stream-level false-alarm rate of SLIDE only loosely (zero of 20 is compatible with a true rate of up to about 0.14 at 95\% confidence), but the comparison with the per-test configurations on the same streams is direct.

\subsection{Real drift without a change of $P(X)$}\label{sec:realdrift}
Table~\ref{tab:rev-real} gives the real-drift probe. As expected from Section~\ref{sec:formulation}, SLIDE raises no alarm: the input distribution does not change, and its R1 performance equals that of the static head (0.710 MCC, recall 0.775). An audit of five random labels per batch, monitored by ADWIN, does not detect the change in any realisation, and neither does an audit of ten. With 25 audit labels per batch, a tenth of the traffic, the audit raises an alarm in 6 of ten realisations, on average 10.5 batches after the change, and lifts R1 MCC to 0.736 at 1{,}035 labels. The fully labelled methods do not do better within the 16 batches of R1. ADWIN-Retrain updates once, in the first or second batch of R1 before any R1 label has arrived, and ends at 0.702. The online TF-IDF learner raises recall to 0.868 but ends at the same MCC (0.710) because it also flags more legitimate mail. A cheap audit therefore does not close the blind spot. Detecting a posterior change that affects about a fifth of the legitimate traffic took a tenth of the labels, and even then only in most realisations. A deployment that must guard against such changes needs a label stream of its own, and the label-free trigger is a complement to it, not a replacement.

\subsection{Authorship slices}\label{sec:slices}
LLM-written legitimate mail is easy to separate from human-written mail by style: a TF-IDF logistic regression tells the two apart with 0.9996 macro-F1 under five-fold cross-validation on the legitimate messages alone. A detector can therefore lower its loss on segment D by learning authorship instead of intent. Table~\ref{tab:rev-slices} measures how much each method does so. SLIDE detects 0.874 of the LLM-written phishing in D and flags 0.166 of the LLM-written legitimate mail, against 0.027 of the human legitimate mail in the same batches. The online TF-IDF learner reaches the same recall at an FPR of 0.044 on that slice, and SLIDE-TFIDF, with the same 3{,}000 labels as SLIDE, at 0.089. The excess FPR is therefore a property of the frozen-encoder head, not of the label budget. Segment F tests the confound directly, because there LLM-written legitimate mail is as frequent as LLM-written phishing and a style rule would flag half of the LLM traffic wrongly. SLIDE, SLIDE-TFIDF, ADWIN-Retrain and Online-TFIDF-SGD keep the F-segment FPR on LLM-written legitimate mail between 0.064 and 0.124 at recall above 0.91, so once legitimate LLM mail is labelled in the purchased window, the detectors do learn to separate it from LLM phishing. The highest FPR on legitimate mail is on the paraphrased segment E, where SLIDE flags 0.205 of the paraphrased legitimate messages and SLIDE-TFIDF 0.050.

\subsection{Benign padding and windowed scoring}\label{sec:stress}
Table~\ref{tab:rev-stress} attacks the end-of-stream detectors with benign text around held-out malicious messages. Paraphrase (T1) and character noise (T2) change little. A benign prefix is the main weakness of every head on the frozen encoder: with 400 characters of prefix SLIDE detects 0.034 of the pool, against 0.658 without it, and SLIDE+replay and the earlier design fare no better (0.067 and 0.068). The TF-IDF learners see the whole message and lose less (0.561 for the online learner, 0.555 for SLIDE-TFIDF), but a 400-character prefix and suffix together (T5) also defeats them (0.278 and 0.307). The second pool, which was never streamed, shows the same pattern (Table~\ref{tab:rev-stress2}): the prefix lowers the detection rate of SLIDE on PhishFuzzer phishing from 0.912 to 0.017.

\input{tables/rev_stress.tex}

Windowed scoring (Section~\ref{sec:chunk}) removes the attack. Chunked SLIDE detects 0.801 of the padded pool, 0.800 with an 800-character prefix and 0.734 under T5, and 0.970 of the padded PhishFuzzer phishing. The price is paid on legitimate mail. On the clean A--G stream the maximum over windows raises the FPR of SLIDE from 0.045 to 0.259 and lowers stream MCC from 0.889 to 0.691, and ADWIN-Retrain shows the same loss (0.693). The head is trained on first windows only and does not know that later windows of a long legitimate message can look suspicious. The maximum rule is therefore a defence to switch on for messages that are long or that start with boilerplate, not a default, and a head trained on all windows is the obvious next step that we have not run.

\subsection{Cost}\label{sec:cost}
The frozen encoder dominates the cost of every head-based method: one pass takes 27\,ms per message on four CPU threads, while the head adds 0.006\,ms. For SLIDE, the drift test costs 29\,s over the 144 batches of a stream (about 198\,ms per batch of 250 messages) and the six updates 109\,s. With the embeddings cached, a whole stream of 36{,}000 messages runs in 163\,s, against 305\,s with replay and 352\,s for ADWIN-Retrain. By comparison, the earlier design needed a second language model for the surprisal features (72\,ms per message), which the revision removes. In contrast, the TF-IDF learners need no encoder at all: scoring costs 0.5\,ms per message and the online learner runs a stream in 30\,s. SLIDE-TFIDF keeps this learner but still needs one encoder pass per message for the drift test, which multiplies the per-message compute of a TF-IDF detector by about 55. What SLIDE saves is labels, not compute.
